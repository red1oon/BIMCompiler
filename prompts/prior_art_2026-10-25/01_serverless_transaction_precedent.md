# No production precedent for fully serverless transactions

**To our knowledge, we found no documented production system that handles real money or regulated transactions with no always-on database server and no always-on application server, where every device holds a full database and signed or append-only events move through a passive store-and-forward transport.** Every system we examined falls into one of two groups. In the first, no single shared central database exists, but each participant or the network still runs servers: Corda, Hyperledger Fabric, Lotus Notes/Domino, CouchDB/PouchDB, Bitcoin, and the peer-to-peer log projects (Secure Scuttlebutt, Holochain, p2panda, Hypercore) all fit here. In the second, devices accept transactions offline, but a central host stays authoritative and settles later: Square, Stripe, Adyen, SumUp, EMV chip cards, ATM stand-in and the Reserve Bank of India offline framework all fit here. What the design needs is a combination of three things: devices that are authoritative for their own records, a byte-only post office, and no validating server anywhere. We did not find that combination at production scale with money. The research does find strong precedent for the ingredients: per-device full replicas, signed append-only logs, store-and-forward carriage, bounded offline risk, and git or object-store compare-and-swap. The sharpest unresolved risks are fork or double-spend from two writers, rollback after a backup restore, and an owner re-signing history. Whether Thai pawnshop or accounting law accepts such a design is unanswered and needs a Thai lawyer. Silence in the sources we could read is not permission. Wording the team can use appears in the final section. Everything below is from research notes dated 2026-10-24; inferences are labelled as such.

The pattern the table makes visible is that the closest "no central database" systems all keep an arbiter. Corda's own docs describe the notary as a network service providing "uniqueness consensus" and "the point of finality", and its options range from a single-node notary to clustered ones, none of which is device-resident ([Corda notaries](https://docs.r3.com/en/platform/corda/4.13/community/key-concepts-notaries.html), via excerpt). Fabric peers each hold an equivalent ledger copy, but ordering belongs to an orderer cluster that becomes unavailable for read and write if quorum is lost ([Fabric ordering service](https://hyperledger-fabric.readthedocs.io/en/latest/orderer/ordering_service.html)). Neither has a dumb post office in its trust model. Our inference is that Corda's non-validating notary, which sees only input references, is the nearest analogy to a minimal arbiter, but it is still an active always-on service.

## The two closest families answer "who arbitrates?" very differently

The replicated-document family (Notes, CouchDB) is the nearest relative of "every device holds a full database," and its honest lesson is that it does not arbitrate scarce resources at all. Domino resolves a same-server save conflict by letting the first save win, but across replicas it keeps both versions and leaves a human to reconcile ([HCL conflicts help](https://help.hcl-software.com/notes/14.0.0/client/rep_save_conflicts_t.html)). CouchDB picks a deterministic winner so every peer sees the same thing, yet states that actually merging "is an application-specific function" and offers no multi-document transactions ([CouchDB conflicts](https://docs.couchdb.org/en/latest/replication/conflicts.html)). Both are proven for 30-plus years at scale for documents; neither shows that two disconnected clerks cannot both sell the last unit. Our inference from those rules is that a deterministic display winner is not a business-correct winner. The production CouchDB case with the best documentation, the Community Health Toolkit, needs the server for authentication and is hub-and-spoke. Its one money-adjacent deployment, performance-based payments to about 2,300 community health workers in Zanzibar, calculated and disbursed payment outside CouchDB, and the implementation report says poor sync "led to payment discrepancies" ([PMC12136488](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12136488/)).

The offline-payment family (Square, Stripe, Adyen, SumUp, EMV) is the nearest relative on risk handling, and it is a bounded exception rather than a different authority model. Square lets a device queue up to 1,000 offline payments for up to 24 hours, expires pending payments at 72 hours on some devices, and says the seller is responsible for expired, declined or disputed payments ([Square SDK](https://developer.squareup.com/docs/mobile-payments-sdk/android/offline-payments); [Square help](https://squareup.com/help/gb/en/article/7775-process-offline-payments-faq)). Adyen states "You are fully liable for failed captures, chargebacks, and disputes on offline payments" ([Adyen](https://docs.adyen.com/point-of-sale/offline-payment)). The portable ideas are per-item caps, cumulative caps, count caps, time limits, forced periodic online contact and limits fetched while online. They apply because the offline party is the one bearing risk. One structural difference, as our inference: in a closed pawn shop the offline risk-taker and the settler are the same entity, so the card model of "merchant bears risk, issuer settles" does not map directly.

A passive transport can also supply arbitration if it offers an atomic primitive. S3 now supports conditional `If-None-Match` and `If-Match` writes, and git hosts reject non-fast-forward pushes, so either can serve as a compare-and-swap on one key ([AWS S3 conditional writes](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/conditional-writes.md)). Our inference is that an email mailbox or plain shared folder offers no such primitive, so with those transports ordering must come from a designated device or be accepted as eventual with later reconciliation. A commentator argues S3 conditional writes cover single-key CAS but "do not replace consensus" for multi-key coordination, which is opinion ([braindetox.kr](https://braindetox.kr/en/posts/s3_does_not_replace_consensus_conditional_writes_2026.html)).

## Sceptic objections that survive the research

The strongest objection is formal. Bailis et al. show uniqueness of inserted values, sequential ID assignment and a "greater-than" threshold under concurrent decrements are not safe to run without coordination, while 10 of 12 TPC-C invariants are ([Bailis et al.](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/1402.2237)). A bank-account example in the CARD paper shows two replicas each seeing balance 10 can both withdraw and push it negative ([CARD paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08733)). Our inference for a pawn system is that one-live-pledge-per-item, redeem-or-forfeit-once, vault balance not below zero and gap-free ticket numbers all fall into the coordination-needed set. The standard mitigations in that literature (partitioned ID namespaces, escrow of rights, a single sequencer) amount to single-writer-per-record, which is a coordination assumption rather than a property of the data structure. The local-first essay that popularised the pattern itself says "We are not talking about implementing things like banking services" ([Ink & Switch](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first/)).

Three specific scenarios survive, and the notes label all three as inferences rather than sourced findings. First, a fork: two counters sharing one signing key, or a device restored from backup, can each append validly signed, conflicting events; a hash chain proves tampering, not agreement, and the literature we read treats detecting forks after the fact as feasible but preventing them as needing a coordinator. Holochain's docs concede a participant can "create a perfectly valid, but wrong, alternate history for themselves" and answer only that others will notice ([Holochain source chain](https://developer.holochain.org/concepts/3_source_chain/)); community material on Secure Scuttlebutt warns that a forked feed cannot be repaired ([Scuttlebot docs](https://scuttlebot.io/more/protocols/secure-scuttlebutt.html), older community material). Second, rollback: restoring an older journal onto a device can silently re-issue ticket numbers already handed to customers, which follows from the sequential-ID result above. Third, owner re-signing: a key-holder can rebuild a fresh, internally consistent chain unless a head hash is anchored externally, for example by regularly sending it to a party the owner does not control. Related losses are documented in a milder form: Actual Budget warns that after losing local data and forgetting the password "you can never recover your data" ([Actual docs](https://actualbudget.org/docs/getting-started/sync/)), and Kleppmann describes malicious-replica resilience and access control in leaderless designs as open research ([Kleppmann keynote](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2025/03/31/papoc-keynote-byzantine.html)). Absence of production postmortems does not show safety: the notes found none for CRDT or local-first systems moving money, but such systems are rarely deployed for money.

## The Thai regulatory question is open, and silence is not permission

We could not retrieve the Pawnshop Act B.E. 2505 provisions on pawn tickets, account books, retention or inspection; the Department of Provincial Administration's unofficial translation returned 403, and only academic and press summaries were available. The confirmed facts are narrow. The Ministry of Interior regulates pawnshops, with the Royal Thai Police Registration Division chief on the control committee ([unofficial translation](https://multi.dopa.go.th/tspd/official_letter/download/2664/EN)). Accounts and supporting documents must be kept at least five years under the Accounting Act, and a Revenue Department order permits electronic keeping ([Revenue Department](https://rd.go.th/25480.html), exact page per statement not individually verified). The personal-data law requires breach notification, where feasible within 72 hours, though a lost device with encrypted personal data is given as a case that may not need reporting ([IAPP](https://www.iapp.org/news/a/thailand-s-pdpc-clarifies-data-breach-notification-requirements)). Anti-money-laundering duties reach gold and jewellery dealers, but thresholds conflict across sources and pawnshop-specific obligations are unconfirmed ([Tilleke & Gibbins](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/thailand-anti-money-laundering)). We found nothing that requires a central server-side record and nothing that blesses a per-device ledger. Our inference is that an inspector may expect a producible, readable register on the premises, that encryption at rest on every device is load-bearing for the privacy argument, and that the answer may differ between a licensed pawnshop and a ขายฝาก (sale with right of redemption) contract under Civil and Commercial Code sections 491 and following ([Legal 500](https://legal500.com/developments/?p=31058)). The treatment of hash-chained signed electronic records under Thai electronic-transactions law was not researched. Bank of Thailand offline-payment rules were searched for and not found. These questions need a Thai lawyer.

## What we can honestly claim, what is unverified, and what was not researched

Safe wording, with the "to our knowledge" qualifiers kept: "To our knowledge, we found no documented production precedent for a money-handling system with no always-on database or application server. We found strong precedent for the ingredients: full local replicas with offline work (Notes since 1989, CouchDB/PouchDB since 2005); signed append-only logs; store-and-forward carriage; bounded offline risk with deferred reconciliation (Square, Stripe, Adyen, SumUp, EMV); and compare-and-swap on a passive store (git hosts, S3 conditional writes). Our design combines these and replaces the central arbiter with a defined rule: one writing device per record, an externally anchored head hash, and caps. We do not claim to be the first." Do not claim "proven in production" for the architecture, do not cite Bitcoin, Corda or Fabric as precedent for serverless operation, and do not claim regulatory acceptance.

For the team, which is unfamiliar with a world without a database server, the suggested framing (our wording, not drawn from any source) is: **each device is its own database; replication of signed events through a mailbox; no master.** The notes support adding two honest qualifiers: the mailbox is a courier, not a record-keeper, and anything shared and scarce still needs a named single owner or an anchored check, because no system we examined removes the arbiter, only moves it.

Unverified items to carry forward: Corda facts come from search excerpts because docs.r3.com and r3.com blocked fetching; Corda end-of-support dates and the current live status of Marco Polo, HQLAx and any 2026 Corda or Fabric production network were not confirmed; that a notary-free Corda transaction issuing without inputs needs no notarisation is unverified design knowledge; Stripe's risk wording and EMV semantics are from summaries and checklists; the RBI cap structure is from press, not the circular; SWIFT's authority point is "to our knowledge" only; whether Notes workstations could replicate with each other without any server is unknown; and the Holochain Unyt and HoloFuel evidence is vendor material. Research gaps deliberately left unresearched here: offline e-purses such as Mondex, Geldkarte and Octopus (the most plausible real precedent for device-held value, which to our knowledge held value on a chip with the issuer settling later, unsourced); eCash and Chaumian systems; central-bank digital currency offline pilots; EDI and AS2 store-and-forward; and SQLite replication tools such as Litestream and cr-sqlite, along with Dropbox-style shared-folder accounting and email-based order systems. These could contain better precedent than anything above.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "has anyone done this without servers?" to "who is the arbiter, and where does it live?" In every system examined the answer is a server, a quorum, a processor or a git host, and the products that go closest to the pawn design (Square, Stripe, Adyen, SumUp) openly assign the divergence risk to the merchant and cap it. A design that removes the central arbiter must therefore state its replacement as a rule the team can test: single writer per pledge and ticket series, an externally anchored head hash, bounded cash and gold exposure per device, and a recovery procedure for restores. Those are design commitments, not precedents.

The most useful next steps are inexpensive. A Thai lawyer should answer the register, retention and inspection question for the actual business type. The team should adopt the fork, rollback and re-signing scenarios as named test cases, and use the externally anchored head hash as the place where a minimal outside party, a mailbox with conditional writes or a periodic notary-like receipt, re-enters the design by choice.
